## 1. Core Notes

### A. Amendments requiring State ratification

- Most constitutional amendments are passed by Parliament through the special-majority procedure discussed in the preceding lecture.
- For specified federal provisions, an amendment additionally requires ratification by **at least half of the State Legislatures**.
- Sequence described:
  1. The Bill is passed **separately by each House of Parliament** by the prescribed special majority.
  2. At least half of the State Legislatures approve it through **simple-majority resolutions**.
  3. After the required State ratifications, the Bill is presented to the President for assent.
- The teacher contrasts India with the USA: every US constitutional amendment requires ratification by **three-fourths of the States**, whereas in India State ratification applies only to specified federal provisions.
- The teacher uses **28 States and 15 approvals** as the illustration. This is retained as the lecture's figure, not asserted as a current count.

**Provisions identified in the transcript as requiring State ratification:**
1. **Method of election of the President** — elected members of State Legislative Assemblies participate in the presidential election.
2. **Extent of executive powers of the Union and States** — changing the constitutional distribution/extent of these powers requires State ratification.
3. **Distribution of legislative powers** between the Union and States.
4. **Any List in the Seventh Schedule** — Union List, State List and Concurrent List.
5. **Representation of States in Parliament** — changing a State's allotted representation in Parliament.
6. **Article 279A** — GST Council; the teacher says it was added through the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016.
7. **Article 368 itself** — alteration of the constitutional amendment procedure.

### B. Fourth feature: No hierarchy among the three organs; checks and balances

- The three organs are the **Legislature, Executive and Judiciary**.
- Each has a defined role and checks the others through **checks and balances**.
- The Constitution does not establish a simple hierarchy in which one organ is inherently superior to the other two.
- The expected relationship is **harmony and balance**, not institutional supremacy.

**Comparison used by the teacher:**
- **United Kingdom — parliamentary supremacy:** The UK is traditionally described as the home of parliamentary sovereignty. The teacher explains that, because the UK does not have a single written Constitution in the Indian sense, primary legislation is not ordinarily struck down by courts for being unconstitutional. The teacher also says public opinion and political culture restrain Parliament in practice.
- **United States — judicial supremacy (as characterised in the lecture):** The US Supreme Court can invalidate legislation that violates the Constitution. The teacher says amending the US Constitution to overcome such a judgment is extremely difficult.
- **India — via media:** India follows a middle path. Parliament can respond to a judicial decision by changing an ordinary law or, where necessary, amending the Constitution. However, an amendment cannot damage the **basic structure**; the Supreme Court can review and invalidate an amendment that violates this limit.

### C. Fifth feature: Political justice along with social and economic justice

- **Political justice** includes:
  - Equality before law / equality in the eyes of law.
  - **Universal adult franchise:** eligible citizens aged 18 or above can vote.
  - The right to contest elections, subject to applicable qualifications.
- The teacher recalls Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's contrast between political equality and persistent social/economic inequality.
- Formal political equality does not automatically remove:
  - The gap between rich and poor.
  - Caste-based inequality.
  - Gender inequality.
- Therefore, the Constitution also seeks **substantive equality** through **social justice and economic justice**.
- Examples given: reservations and benefits for **SCs, STs, OBCs and EWS**.
- **Formal equality** treats people equally in law; **substantive equality** recognises unequal starting conditions and seeks to reduce disadvantage.

### D. Sixth feature: Federal system with a strong Centre

- India has a **federal structure** with at least two levels of government.
- Each level has defined spheres of action and responsibilities and enjoys a degree of autonomy within its sphere.
- However, the Union is comparatively stronger in legislative, administrative and financial powers.
- The teacher uses the expression **“federal system with a unitary bias”** to describe this tilt towards the Centre.
- Detailed explanation is deferred to the chapter on Centre–State relations.

### E. Seventh feature: Westminster parliamentary system with an elected head of State

- India follows the **Westminster system of parliamentary government**, borrowed from the UK.
- The key difference highlighted is India's **elected head of State**.
- In the UK example used by the teacher, the Crown (King/Queen) is hereditary and the position passes through succession.
- India combines the Westminster parliamentary system with an elected head of State.

### F. Eighth feature: Justiciable and non-justiciable provisions/rights

- **Justiciable rights:** Fundamental Rights in **Part III** can be enforced through courts. The teacher notes that a person may approach a High Court or directly the Supreme Court for enforcement.
- **Non-justiciable provisions:** Directive Principles in **Part IV** are described in the lecture as non-justiciable.
- The teacher questions whether “non-justiciable rights” is a contradiction in terms: if an entitlement cannot be enforced in court, calling it a right may be conceptually problematic.
- The detailed relationship between Parts III and IV is deferred.
- The transcript uses “non-justiciable rights” for Directive Principles; retain the distinction that the lecture is discussing their non-enforceability in court.

### G. Ninth feature: Emergency provisions

The teacher identifies three kinds of emergency:

- **Article 352** — National Emergency.
- **Article 356** — Constitutional breakdown in a State / President's Rule.
- **Article 360** — Financial Emergency.

- The teacher explains Article 356 as a situation where the State government is not being carried on in accordance with the Constitution, allowing the Union to intervene and the State government to be displaced.
- The teacher contrasts this with the US system, saying an equivalent power for the US President to dismiss State Governors would be unacceptable there.
- Detailed treatment and comparison are deferred.

## 2. Teacher's Warnings and Traps

No explicit “be careful”, “books wrongly say” or “don't confuse” warning appears in this segment. The following are distinctions the teacher explicitly emphasises:

- State ratification is required only for **specified federal provisions**, not every constitutional amendment.
- State Legislatures ratify through **simple-majority resolutions**, unlike Parliament's special-majority requirement.
- Checks and balances do not mean that one organ is automatically superior to the others.
- India is described as a **via media** between parliamentary supremacy and judicial supremacy.
- Political equality alone does not ensure social and economic equality.
- India has an elected head of State, unlike the hereditary British Crown.
- The teacher questions the expression “non-justiciable rights” while referring to Directive Principles.
