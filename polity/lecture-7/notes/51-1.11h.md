# Schedules of the Constitution and Constitutional Oaths — UPSC Notes

**One-line summary:** Constitutional Schedules are supporting attachments to specified Articles or Parts; the First lists States and Union Territories, the Second deals with service-related matters of specified dignitaries, and the Third contains forms of oaths and affirmations.

## 1. Topic + One-line Summary

The lecture closes the discussion on **basic features**, introduces the meaning and purpose of constitutional **Schedules**, and explains the First, Second and Third Schedules. It then discusses the meaning and wording of constitutional oaths and the limits on deviations from the prescribed form.

## 2. Core Notes

### A. Closing point: Basic Features vs Basic Structure

- **Basic features** are distilled from the Constitution by political writers and constitutional experts, based on their perspectives.
- The **Constituent Assembly did not provide an authoritative list** of basic features.
- Different writers may include or exclude different features.
- **Basic features** and the **Basic Structure doctrine** are separate concepts.

### B. Meaning and purpose of Schedules

- A **Schedule** is a supporting attachment to specified Articles or Parts of the Constitution.
- The teacher describes it as an explanatory note, footnote, memorandum, addendum or attachment.
- Schedules are not merely attachments to the Constitution as a whole; each is linked to particular Articles or Parts.
- The relevant Articles or Parts are identified at the beginning of each Schedule in the bare Act.
- Ordinary Acts passed by Parliament may also contain Schedules at the end, where supporting details are placed separately from the main provisions.

**Teacher's illustration — First Schedule**
- **Part I, Articles 1–4** deals with the territory of India, including formation and reorganisation of States and organisation of Union Territories.
- These provisions need a corresponding list identifying the States and Union Territories. The **First Schedule** supplies that list.
- Therefore, the First Schedule is attached to **Articles 1 and 4** and supports the territorial provisions in Part I.

### C. First Schedule — States and Union Territories

- Contains the names of the **States and Union Territories**.
- The teacher says the Schedule also records the year of formation of a State and, where applicable, later changes to its boundaries or the creation of a new State from it.
- It can therefore be consulted for the chronology of State formation and reorganisation.
- The teacher says this topic will be revisited in detail in the chapter on the **territory of India**.

### D. Second Schedule — Emoluments and conditions of service

- Deals primarily with **salaries, emoluments, privileges and conditions of service** of specified constitutional dignitaries.
- The teacher lists:
  - President and Vice-President.
  - Governors.
  - Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  - Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG).
  - Presiding officers of Parliament: Speaker, Deputy Speaker, Chairman and Deputy Chairman/Deputy Chairperson.
  - Corresponding State presiding officers: Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Legislative Assembly; where a State has a Legislative Council, its Chairman and Deputy Chairman.
- The teacher describes the Schedule as almost redundant in practical terms, but its subject is the service-related provisions for these offices.

### E. Oaths and affirmations — meaning

- The Constitution prescribes different forms of oath or affirmation for different constitutional positions because their **duties and responsibilities differ**.
- **Oath:** A commitment that gives:
  1. A **general direction** — the common commitment found across oaths.
  2. A **specific direction** — the particular commitment associated with the office being assumed.
- The **incumbent** is the person who holds or is taking up the constitutional position.

### F. Third Schedule — Forms of oath or affirmation

- The Third Schedule contains prescribed forms of oaths and affirmations.
- The lecture begins by reading the form for a **Union Minister**.
- The prescribed opening allows the person to:
  - “swear in the name of God”; or
  - “solemnly affirm”.
- The transcript records the teacher's historical observation that some Constituent Assembly members, including Nehru and Ambedkar, were not keen on references to God in the Constitution or oaths; the final text nevertheless includes the alternative wording.
- The teacher discusses whether a person taking an oath may depart from the prescribed form.

**Permitted wording variations discussed in the lecture**
- The teacher says courts have accepted equivalent translations of “God” in the language in which the oath is taken, giving examples such as **Ishwar**, **Allah** and **Deva**.
- The teacher distinguishes these translations from replacing the prescribed reference with a different object of allegiance or inspiration.
- Examples of disputed deviations mentioned include taking an oath in the name of **Gomatha**, **farmers**, or a particular spiritual guru or historical personality.
- The teacher's central point is that an oath-taking person should follow the form prescribed in the Constitution; the exact legal consequence of a deviation is not fully explained in the supplied transcript.

## 3. Teacher's Warnings and Traps

- Do not confuse **basic features** with the **Basic Structure doctrine**; the teacher expressly says they are separate concepts.
- Do not call a Schedule merely an attachment to the whole Constitution: it is attached to specified Articles or Parts.
- Do not confuse the everyday meaning of “schedule” with the constitutional use of the term.
- The teacher cautions against deviating from the prescribed oath by substituting a different object or personality for the wording provided in the Constitution.
