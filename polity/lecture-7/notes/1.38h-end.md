# UPSC Polity Notes: Sixth, Seventh and Eighth Schedules; Language Recognition

**One-line summary:** The lecture introduces the Sixth Schedule’s tribal-area arrangements, explains how the Seventh Schedule distributes legislative subjects between the Union and States, and examines the Eighth Schedule, language-inclusion demands, Articles 344 and 351, and the separate recognition of classical languages.

---

## 1. Core Notes

### A. Sixth Schedule — tribal areas in four North-Eastern States
- Provides for the **administration of tribal areas** in **Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram**.
- The teacher flags the four-State list as a likely Prelims fact.
- The Schedule is lengthy and has generated implementation-related issues and controversies; detailed treatment is deferred to the separate topic on administration of North-Eastern tribal areas.

### B. Seventh Schedule — distribution of legislative powers
- Contains three legislative lists:
  - **Union List:** Parliament legislates on these subjects.
  - **State List:** State Legislatures legislate on these subjects.
  - **Concurrent List:** Both Parliament and State Legislatures can legislate.
- The teacher’s organising principle for allocation:
  1. **Union List:** nationwide uniformity is essential.
  2. **Concurrent List:** nationwide uniformity may be desirable, but is not essential.
  3. **State List:** nationwide uniformity is neither desirable nor essential; laws should allow local adaptation to each State’s socio-economic and political conditions.
- Subject counts quoted in the lecture are historical/date-sensitive; see **Additions** and **Error Log**.

### C. Transfer of subjects by the 42nd Amendment
- The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** transferred five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List:
  1. Education
  2. Forests
  3. Weights and measures
  4. Protection of wild animals and birds
  5. Administration of justice and organisation of subordinate courts
- In Centre–State relations discussions, **“transferred items”** generally refers to these five subjects.
- The **Punchhi Commission** recommended that these subjects be restored to the State List. The teacher deferred the rationale to the later chapter on Centre–State relations.

### D. Eighth Schedule — language recognition
- The teacher discusses the difficulty of deciding which languages should be included:
  - The number of speakers should be **sizable**, but the term has no precise threshold in the lecture.
  - Government must consider whether a claimed language is distinct or a dialect of another language.
  - A dialect may evolve over time and acquire features of a separate language; the distinction can be difficult.
- The teacher refers to approximately **38 inclusion demands [DATED - verify current]**, giving examples such as Khasi, Himachali Pahari and Bundelkhandi.
- Two earlier attempts to frame criteria are named:
  - **Pahwa Committee (1996)**
  - **Sitakant Mahapatra Committee (2003)**
  Their attempts to establish workable criteria were described as inconclusive.

### E. Benefits / constitutional references connected with the Eighth Schedule
1. **Article 344**
   - Provides for a Commission on Official Language, with members representing the languages specified in the Eighth Schedule.
   - The teacher explains the constitutional timing as five years and thereafter ten years from commencement: 1955 and 1960.
   - The teacher says the first Commission was set up, but the second was not; he therefore questions how tangible this benefit is.
2. **Article 351**
   - Places a duty on the Union to promote the spread of Hindi and develop/enrich it.
   - The teacher’s explanation: languages develop partly by assimilating vocabulary and expressions from other languages; English is used as an example of borrowing from Persian, Arabic, Spanish, French and Latin.
   - Hindi may draw expressions from **Hindustani** and the other Eighth Schedule languages.
   - **Priority:** Article 351 says Hindi’s vocabulary should draw **primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on the other languages**.
   - The teacher describes Hindustani as a blend or “golden mean” between Hindi and Urdu, illustrating ordinary mixed usage with *intezaar* and the more Sanskritised *pratiksha*.

### F. Classical languages — a category separate from the Eighth Schedule
- Classical-language status is **not provided in the Constitution**; the teacher describes it as a Government of India recognition introduced in 2004.
- The teacher’s historical sequence:
  - Tamil — 2004
  - Sanskrit — 2005
  - Telugu and Kannada — 2008
  - Malayalam — 2013
  - Odia — 2014
  This is the position described in the lecture and is now **[DATED - verify current]**.
- Criteria explained by the teacher:
  1. **High antiquity:** early texts / recorded history dating back roughly 1,500–2,000 years.
  2. **Original literary tradition:** not borrowed from another speech community.
  3. **Large body of literary texts:** not merely one or two surviving works.
  4. **Distinct classical literature:** classical literature should be distinguishable from the language’s modern literature.
- Recognition is not automatic merely because a language appears to meet criteria; Government recognition is required.
- Benefits described:
  1. Two international awards each year for leading scholars of the language.
  2. Government assistance for a **Centre of Excellence** for the language.
  3. UGC support for **professional chairs** in the language at Central Universities.

## 2. Teacher’s Warnings and Traps

- **Do not mix up the four Sixth Schedule States:** Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram.
- For the Seventh Schedule, distinguish the three tests: Union = uniformity essential; Concurrent = desirable but not essential; State = neither desirable nor essential, with local adaptation needed.
- Do not confuse the five **transferred items** with subjects originally placed in the Concurrent List.
- The teacher specifically highlights that many textbooks give the original list counts but may omit the changes made by the **101st Amendment / GST**.
- Do not confuse the Eighth Schedule with classical-language status: classical-language recognition is not in the Constitution.
- Under Article 351, **Sanskrit is the primary source** for enriching Hindi vocabulary; other Eighth Schedule languages are secondary sources.
- The teacher flags the classical-language chronology as a possible Prelims question, especially the first language and the last one in the list as it stood in the lecture. That list is now dated.

## 7. Additions (Not from Lecture)

- **[+] [DATED - verify current] The Eighth Schedule currently contains 22 languages.** This is useful for current Prelims revision; the Ministry of Home Affairs lists the constitutional provisions under Articles 344(1) and 351 and the 22 languages. Source: Ministry of Home Affairs official document. [C1]
- **[+] [DATED - verify current] India currently recognises 11 classical languages:** Tamil, Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Odia, Marathi, Assamese, Bengali, Pali and Prakrit. Five languages—Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali—were added in October 2024. This updates the lecture’s six-language list ending with Odia in 2014. Source: Ministry of Culture / Government of India press release and Gazette notification. [C2]
- **[+] The revised 2024 classical-language criteria include:** high antiquity of early texts/recorded history over 1,500–2,000 years; a body of ancient literature considered heritage by generations; knowledge texts including prose as well as poetry and epigraphical/inscriptional evidence; and the possibility that classical literature may be distinct from, or discontinuous with, later forms. This is more detailed than the four criteria as orally summarised in the lecture. Source: Gazette of India notification, 4 October 2024. [C3]
- **[+] The Government has stated that there are no fixed criteria for inclusion in the Eighth Schedule**, and has described the Pahwa (1996) and Sitakant Mahapatra (2003) Committee attempts as inconclusive. This supports the lecture’s account while clarifying the Government’s stated position. Source: Lok Sabha written answer, 11 February 2025. [C4]
