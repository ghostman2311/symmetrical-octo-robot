## 4. Mains Practice Questions and Model Answers

### Q1. Explain the criticism that the distribution of legislative subjects may weaken State autonomy. What arguments qualify this criticism?
**Word limit: 250**

**Intro:** The Seventh Schedule allocates legislative subjects between the Union and States through three lists. Its design attempts to balance national coordination with regional autonomy.

**Body:**

**Centralisation:** The Union List reserves subjects requiring nationwide uniformity to Parliament. This supports common national standards but concentrates legislative authority at the Union level.

**Shared jurisdiction:** The Concurrent List permits both levels to legislate where uniformity may be desirable but is not essential. This can facilitate coordination, although it may also reduce the exclusive space available to States.

**Transfer of subjects:** The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 moved five subjects—including education, forests and weights and measures—from the State List to the Concurrent List.

**Federal correction:** The Punchhi Commission recommended restoring these five subjects to the State List, reflecting concerns about State-level responsibility and local needs.

**Qualification:** The State List is intended for subjects where uniformity is neither necessary nor desirable, allowing laws to respond to local socio-economic and political conditions.

**Conclusion:** The distribution has a centralising dimension, but it also recognises State-specific diversity. Its federal balance depends on preserving meaningful space for local adaptation.

**Keywords to hit:** Seventh Schedule; legislative lists; uniformity; local adaptation; 42nd Amendment; Punchhi Commission.  
**Common mistake:** Do not say the 42nd Amendment transferred all five subjects to the Union List; they were moved to the Concurrent List.

### Q2. What is the difference between the Eighth Schedule and the recognition of a classical language?
**Word limit: 150**

**Intro:** The Eighth Schedule is part of the Constitution, whereas classical-language status is a separate recognition granted by the Government.

**Body:**

**Constitutional status:** The Eighth Schedule lists languages recognised in the constitutional framework; the lecture links it to Articles 344 and 351.

**Purpose described:** Article 344 provides for representation of Eighth Schedule language speakers in the Official Language Commission; Article 351 concerns enrichment of Hindi through specified language sources.

**Separate recognition:** Classical-language status is not mentioned in the Constitution. The teacher traces this category to 2004, beginning with Tamil.

**Criteria:** The lecture identifies antiquity, original literary tradition, a large body of texts and distinct classical literature as the criteria discussed.

**Conclusion:** Inclusion in the Eighth Schedule and classical-language recognition are different classifications, with different bases and purposes.

**Keywords to hit:** Eighth Schedule; Articles 344 and 351; classical language; constitutional status; antiquity; literary tradition.  
**Common mistake:** Do not assume that every Eighth Schedule language is automatically a classical language.

### Q3. “Language development requires both preservation of linguistic diversity and enrichment through exchange, but Hindi receives a specific constitutional direction.” Discuss.
**Word limit: 150**

**Intro:** Article 351 directs the Union to promote Hindi while recognising the contribution of other Indian languages to its enrichment.

**Body:**

**Promotion:** The Union has a duty to promote the spread and development of Hindi.

**Assimilation:** The teacher explains that languages grow by assimilating vocabulary and expressions from other languages, using English as an illustration.

**Hindustani:** Article 351 refers to forms, styles and expressions used in Hindustani and in other languages specified in the Eighth Schedule.

**Priority:** The constitutional direction is to draw vocabulary primarily from Sanskrit and secondarily from the other languages.

**Conclusion:** Article 351 combines the promotion of Hindi with linguistic borrowing, while prescribing a specific order of priority for vocabulary enrichment.

**Keywords to hit:** Article 351; Union duty; Hindustani; Eighth Schedule; Sanskrit; vocabulary enrichment.  
**Common mistake:** Do not reverse the priority: Sanskrit is primary; other Eighth Schedule languages are secondary.

### Q4. Does the inclusion of a language in the Eighth Schedule guarantee substantial benefits to its speakers? Explain critically.
**Word limit: 250**

**Intro:** The lecture questions whether Eighth Schedule inclusion produces concrete benefits, examining Articles 344 and 351.

**Body:**

**Representation under Article 344:** Speakers of Eighth Schedule languages are represented in the Official Language Commission. The teacher notes that the constitutional timetable referred to commissions after five and ten years from commencement.

**Limited practical effect:** The teacher states that the first Commission was appointed, while the second was not, and therefore questions the practical value of this provision as a recurring benefit.

**Enrichment under Article 351:** The Union is directed to enrich Hindi by drawing on Hindustani and other Eighth Schedule languages, with primary reliance on Sanskrit.

**Recognition difficulty:** Inclusion demands raise difficult questions about the number of speakers and whether a claimed language is distinct from a dialect. The Pahwa and Sitakant Mahapatra Committees did not establish conclusive criteria.

**Qualification:** The Schedule does have constitutional relevance through Articles 344 and 351, even if the teacher considers the direct benefits to speakers limited.

**Conclusion:** Inclusion has constitutional significance, but the lecture does not establish a broad set of automatic individual benefits. Its practical impact must be assessed provision by provision.

**Keywords to hit:** Article 344; Article 351; representation; Official Language Commission; language-dialect distinction; inclusion criteria.  
**Common mistake:** Do not claim Article 351 gives each Eighth Schedule language equal priority in Hindi vocabulary development.
