

## 5. Mains Practice Questions

### Q1. Explain the criticism that a ministerial oath of secrecy is inconsistent with transparent government. What arguments qualify this criticism?

**Model answer (maximum 250 words)**

**Introduction:** The oath of secrecy requires Ministers to protect information obtained in office, while democratic governance also demands openness and accountability.

**Body**

**Criticism — Transparency:** In an information age, a broad secrecy obligation may appear inconsistent with citizens’ demand for open and accountable government.

**Qualification — Confidentiality:** Ministers may handle sensitive information concerning national security, law and order and international affairs.

**Qualification — Limited disclosure:** The oath itself permits communication where it is required for the due discharge of ministerial duties.

**Reform proposal:** The Second ARC recommended replacing the oath of secrecy with an oath of transparency.

**Conclusion:** Confidentiality is necessary for legitimate State functions, but it should not become a blanket shield against accountability. The debate is about reconciling justified secrecy with transparent governance.

**Keywords to hit:** Oath of secrecy, transparency, accountability, national security, Second ARC, ministerial duties.

**Common mistake:** Treating transparency as requiring disclosure of every confidential matter.

### Q2. What is the difference between the Fourth Schedule’s approach to Rajya Sabha representation and the US Senate model?

**Model answer (maximum 150 words)**

**Introduction:** The two systems differ in whether States receive equal representation in the Upper House.

**Body**

- **US Senate:** Each of the 50 States sends two Senators, giving 100 members irrespective of population or area.
- **Equal representation:** Every State has the same number of seats in the US Senate.
- **Indian Rajya Sabha:** State representation is unequal and broadly linked to population.
- **Example:** The lecture cites Uttar Pradesh with 31 seats and Goa with one.
- **Recommendation:** The Punchhi Commission (2007) recommended considering equal State representation in India’s Upper House.

**Conclusion:** The US model gives States equal representation, whereas the Indian model gives States unequal, population-linked representation.

**Keywords to hit:** Fourth Schedule, Rajya Sabha, US Senate, equal representation, population, Punchhi Commission.

**Common mistake:** Assuming every Indian State sends the same number of Rajya Sabha members.

### Q3. “Constitutional oaths contain common commitments, but their wording varies with the responsibilities of each office.” Discuss.

**Model answer (maximum 150 words)**

**Introduction:** Constitutional oaths affirm allegiance to the Constitution, while office-specific wording reflects the duties attached to different positions.

**Body**

**Common commitment:** Oaths commonly require true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and upholding India’s sovereignty and integrity.

**Minister-specific duty:** A Minister promises to discharge ministerial duties faithfully and act without fear or favour, affection or ill-will.

**Additional obligation:** A Minister also takes an oath of secrecy because the office involves access to confidential information.

**Separate constitutional provisions:** The President’s oath is under Article 60, the Vice-President’s under Article 69 and the Governor’s under Article 159.

**Conclusion:** Common constitutional loyalty unites the oaths, while their specific commitments reflect the different responsibilities of each office.

**Keywords to hit:** Constitutional oath, allegiance, minister, secrecy, Article 60, Article 159.

**Common mistake:** Assuming all constitutional functionaries take identical oaths.

### Q4. Explain the constitutional focus of the Fifth and Sixth Schedules. Why should their territorial scope be distinguished?

**Model answer (maximum 250 words)**

**Introduction:** The Fifth and Sixth Schedules provide distinct constitutional frameworks concerning the administration of Scheduled Areas and tribal areas.

**Body**

**Fifth Schedule:** Linked to Article 244(1), it concerns the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes.

**Sixth Schedule:** It concerns the administration of tribal areas in four north-eastern States: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram.

**Different scope:** The Fifth Schedule is framed around Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes, while the Sixth Schedule specifically identifies tribal areas in the four named States.

**Need for distinction:** The schedules are separate constitutional arrangements; their territorial coverage should not be treated as interchangeable.

**Conclusion:** Both schedules concern tribal administration, but they have distinct constitutional scopes. The four-State list is a key identifying feature of the Sixth Schedule.

**Keywords to hit:** Fifth Schedule, Sixth Schedule, Article 244(1), Scheduled Areas, tribal areas, north-east.

**Common mistake:** Confusing the four Sixth Schedule States with the scope of the Fifth Schedule.
