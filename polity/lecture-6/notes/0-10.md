# Basic Features of the Indian Constitution — UPSC Notes

## 1. Lengthiest Written Constitution

The Indian Constitution is one of the world's lengthiest written constitutions.

| Feature   | At commencement | Present position            |
| --------- | --------------- | --------------------------- |
| Articles  | 395             | Around 450 (commonly cited) |
| Parts     | 22              | 25                          |
| Schedules | 8               | 12                          |

UPSC tip: Article counts can vary depending on how inserted and omitted articles are counted. Focus on the original figures and the broad present position.

### Changes in the Parts of the Constitution

Four new Parts were added:

- Part IVA: Fundamental Duties — 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.
- Part IX: Panchayats — 73rd Amendment Act, 1992.
- Part IXA: Municipalities — 74th Amendment Act, 1992.
- Part XIVA: Tribunals — 42nd Amendment Act, 1976.

Two Parts were removed:

- Part VII: Repealed by the 7th Amendment Act, 1956.
- Part IX: The original Part IX was repealed by the 7th Amendment Act, 1956. A new Part IX dealing with Panchayats was later inserted by the 73rd Amendment Act, 1992.

Therefore:

22 original Parts + 4 additions − 1 net removal = 25 Parts.

## 2. Borrowed Constitution

The Indian Constitution is often described as a **borrowed Constitution** because its framers adopted features from several constitutions and adapted them to Indian conditions.

This led to criticism that it was merely a copy of Western constitutions. However, borrowing constitutional ideas does not mean copying them without modification.

### Features Borrowed from the British Constitution (UK)

The largest number of commonly identified constitutional features were drawn from the British system.

| Feature                    | Explanation                                                       |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Parliamentary government   | Westminster model of parliamentary government                     |
| Cabinet system             | Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister                 |
| Collective responsibility  | Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha |
| Bicameralism               | Two Houses at the Union level                                     |
| Parliamentary privileges   | Rights and privileges of Parliament and its members               |
| Single citizenship         | One citizenship for the entire country                            |
| Writs                      | Constitutional remedies involving five traditional writs          |
| Rule of Law                | Associated with A.V. Dicey's theory                               |
| First-Past-the-Post (FPTP) | Candidate receiving the highest number of votes wins              |
| Legislative procedure      | Parliamentary procedures and practices                            |
| Office of Speaker          | Institution and role of the Speaker                               |

**Important distinction:** The British monarch is a hereditary nominal head, whereas India's President is an elected constitutional head.

### First-Past-the-Post (FPTP) System

Under this system, the candidate who receives the **highest number of votes** wins the election.

Example:

| Candidate | Votes           |
| --------- | --------------- |
| A         | 11              |
| B         | 10              |
| C         | 9               |
| D         | 8               |
| Others    | Remaining votes |

Candidate A wins because they received more votes than any other candidate.

**UPSC point:** A candidate does not need to secure more than 50% of the total votes. Winning requires only the highest number of votes among the contestants.

This system is used in direct elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.

### Five Constitutional Writs

The five traditional writs are:

1. **Habeas Corpus:** Protects against unlawful detention.
2. **Mandamus:** Commands a public authority to perform a legal duty.
3. **Prohibition:** Prevents a lower court or tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction.
4. **Certiorari:** Quashes an order of a lower court or tribunal when legally justified.
5. **Quo Warranto:** Questions the legal authority of a person holding a public office.

- Article 32 empowers the Supreme Court to issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
- Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for Fundamental Rights and other legal purposes.

## Quick Revision

- Original Constitution: 395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules.
- Present structure: 25 Parts and 12 Schedules.
- Part IVA: Fundamental Duties.
- Part IX: Panchayats.
- Part IXA: Municipalities.
- Part XIVA: Tribunals.
- Part VII: Repealed by the 7th Amendment Act, 1956.
- Parliamentary government, single citizenship and Rule of Law: British influence.
- FPTP: Candidate with the highest votes wins, even without an absolute majority.
- Five writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto.
