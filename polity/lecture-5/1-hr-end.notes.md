# Criticism of the Indian Constitution & Ambedkar’s Warnings — UPSC Notes

## 1. Why Is the Indian Constitution So Lengthy?

The Constitution is often criticised for being unusually detailed. Major reasons include:

1. **Borrowed constitutional experience:** It drew on provisions and institutional practices from several constitutions.
2. **Administrative detail:** It covers subjects such as public services, elections, audit, languages and Centre–State relations.
3. **Clarity and precision:** Detailed provisions can reduce ambiguity, though they cannot eliminate litigation.
4. **India’s diversity:** Special provisions were needed to address regional, social and historical differences.
5. **One Constitution for the Union and states:** Unlike some federations, India generally has one constitutional framework for both levels.
6. **Influence of the Government of India Act, 1935:** It substantially influenced the Constitution’s administrative and federal structure.
7. **Additional policy provisions:** Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs) and Fundamental Duties add to its scope.

**Balanced view:** Length can make a Constitution complex, but detail may also help govern a diverse country. Avoid treating “long” as automatically “bad.”

## 2. Criticism: The Constitution Is Not Sufficiently Gandhian

Gandhi favoured village-centred self-government, cottage industries and decentralised economic organisation. The Constitution’s original framework, however, gave substantial authority to the Union and states and supported a modern administrative state.

**Counterpoints:**
- Article 40 directs the state to organise village panchayats.
- Article 43 encourages cottage industries in rural areas.
- Article 47 addresses public health and discourages intoxicating drinks and harmful drugs, subject to its wording.
- Article 48 directs the state to organise agriculture and animal husbandry and take steps to prohibit the slaughter of specified cattle.

These provisions are in **Part IV: Directive Principles of State Policy**. DPSPs guide governance but are not directly enforceable by courts (Article 37).

**Local government later gained stronger constitutional status:** The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992) added Part IX on Panchayats and Part IX-A on Municipalities.

## 3. Criticism: Lack of Theoretical Consistency

The Constitution combines values that may appear to be in tension:

- **Modern ideals:** liberty, equality, fraternity and individual rights.
- **Tradition and pluralism:** personal laws and community-specific practices.
- **Individual and group protections:** rights are framed both for individuals and, in certain contexts, groups or minorities.
- **Religious freedom and social reform:** Articles 25–26 protect religious freedom and denominational rights, while Article 25 permits state regulation subject to constitutional limits, including social welfare and reform.

**Evaluation:** These tensions can be understood as an attempt to accommodate India’s diversity and competing social values. They also create continuing debates about equality, religious freedom, personal laws and the role of the state.

## 4. How the Assembly Handled Contentious Issues

The transcript highlights three broad approaches:

1. **Defer resolution:** Leave some difficult questions to future political and social processes. Language was a major example: the Constitution provided for the Union’s official language rather than declaring a “national language.”
2. **Use carefully framed or qualified provisions:** Religious freedom was protected, but the scope of state regulation and reform remained subject to constitutional interpretation.
3. **Place policy goals in the DPSPs:** Some contested goals—such as a Uniform Civil Code (Article 44), prohibition of intoxicating drinks (Article 47), and organisation of village panchayats (Article 40)—were placed among principles that guide the state but are not directly enforceable in court.

**Important language fact:** Article 343 names Hindi in Devanagari script as the Union’s official language. English continued to be used for Union official purposes under the Official Languages Act, 1963, as amended. The Constitution does **not** designate a national language.

## 5. Ambedkar’s Warnings — Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949

### A. Use constitutional methods
Ambedkar warned against relying on civil disobedience, non-cooperation and satyagraha when constitutional methods are available. He called such extra-constitutional methods the **“Grammar of Anarchy.”** This was a warning about normalising them, not a claim that peaceful protest can never have a role.

### B. Avoid hero-worship in politics
He cautioned against placing political leaders above scrutiny. Gratitude and respect must not come at the cost of liberty or democratic institutions. Democracy requires questioning leaders and holding them accountable.

### C. Build social and economic democracy
Political democracy—equal voting rights—is not enough by itself. Ambedkar argued that it must rest on **social democracy**, based on liberty, equality and fraternity. Persistent caste, gender and economic inequalities can undermine political equality.

### D. Respect constitutional morality
Following the Constitution’s literal text is essential, but its institutions must also be operated in the spirit of constitutional values. Ambedkar argued that constitutional morality must be cultivated; it cannot simply be assumed to arise naturally.

**Key idea:** A Constitution’s success depends not only on its text, but also on the people and institutions responsible for working it.

## Quick Revision

- **Article 37:** DPSPs are not enforceable by courts but are fundamental in governance.
- **Article 40:** Village panchayats.
- **Article 43:** Cottage industries.
- **Article 44:** Uniform Civil Code as a DPSP.
- **Article 47:** Public health and prohibition-related direction.
- **Article 48:** Agriculture, animal husbandry and specified cattle.
- **Article 343:** Hindi in Devanagari script as the Union’s official language; it does not declare a national language.
- **73rd Amendment:** Panchayats; **74th Amendment:** Municipalities.
- **Ambedkar’s four cautions:** constitutional methods, no hero-worship, social democracy, constitutional morality.
