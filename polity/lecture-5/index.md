# Criticisms of the Constituent Assembly and the Indian Constitution

**UPSC Polity Notes \| Lecture: 00:00--60:00**

## 1. Criticism: The Constituent Assembly Was Unrepresentative

### Why was it criticised?

-   Members were **indirectly elected** by the Provincial Legislative
    Assemblies, rather than directly elected by all adult citizens.
-   Representatives of the **Princely States were nominated**, not
    elected by their populations.
-   Therefore, critics argued that the Assembly did not directly
    represent the entire Indian electorate.

### Counterarguments

-   Direct elections would have required considerable time and could
    have delayed the transfer of power.
-   The Assembly invited public feedback on the Draft Constitution;
    comments and suggestions were considered during its preparation.
-   Representation can mean more than simply mirroring public opinion:
    members may represent diverse interests and deliberate on behalf of
    the people.
-   The first general elections of **1951--52**, held on the basis of
    universal adult franchise, provided an important democratic test of
    the new constitutional system's public acceptance. They were **not a
    formal referendum on the Constitution**.

**Exam cue:** The Assembly was indirectly elected, but its legitimacy
can also be assessed through deliberation, public consultation, and the
subsequent democratic process.

## 2. Criticism: The Assembly Was Congress-Dominated

### Why was it criticised?

-   The Indian National Congress held a dominant majority in the
    Assembly.
-   Critics argued that this limited political diversity and made the
    Constitution largely a Congress product.

### Counterarguments

-   Congress was a **broad political coalition**, not a group with one
    uniform ideology.
-   It included people with different political, social, economic, and
    philosophical views.
-   Debate within the Assembly and the contribution of members from
    different backgrounds helped shape the final text.

**Key phrase:** Congress dominance did not necessarily mean complete
ideological uniformity.

## 3. Criticism: The Assembly Was Hindu-Dominated

### Why was it criticised?

-   Hindus formed the majority of India's population and also the
    majority of Assembly members.
-   Critics questioned whether minority interests would be adequately
    protected.

### Counterarguments

-   A member's religious identity does not automatically determine the
    nature of a Constitution.
-   The Constitution established a democratic republic and protected
    equality, religious freedom, and minority rights.
-   The character of the Constitution should be assessed through its
    principles and provisions, not only through the religious
    composition of the Assembly.

**Exam cue:** Distinguish the demographic composition of the Assembly
from the constitutional values it adopted.

## 4. Criticism: The Constitution Was Borrowed or a "Carbon Copy"

### Why was it criticised?

-   Several provisions were drawn from other constitutional systems.
-   The Government of India Act, **1935**, strongly influenced parts of
    the Constitution's administrative and federal structure.
-   Critics described the Constitution as imitative rather than
    original.

### Counterarguments

-   Constitutional borrowing can be a deliberate way to learn from
    tested institutions; it does not automatically mean blind copying.
-   Borrowed provisions were adapted to India's circumstances, needs,
    and political objectives.
-   Many constitutional principles---such as rights, representative
    government, and checks on power---are not the exclusive property of
    any one country.
-   India's freedom struggle and earlier constitutional debates had
    already developed demands for representative government and civil
    liberties.
-   The Constitution-makers were working in the mid-20th century, when
    examples from other countries were available to study.
-   A workable Constitution based on tested experience could be
    preferable to an entirely novel but untested design.

**Key distinction:** Borrowing a provision is not the same as copying it
without adaptation.

## 5. Intelligent Adaptation of Borrowed Provisions

-   The Constitution-makers selected and modified ideas to suit Indian
    conditions.
-   For example, India's **Fundamental Rights** in Part III were
    influenced by the US Bill of Rights, but the two systems are not
    identical.
-   India does not have a general constitutional right equivalent to the
    US Second Amendment's individual right to keep and bear arms.
-   Therefore, similarities between constitutions should be assessed
    alongside their differences and local adaptations.

## 6. What Makes a Constitution "Indian"?

-   Criticising the Constitution as "un-Indian" raises a basic question:
    **what exactly defines Indianness?**
-   India contains many languages, religions, cultures, social groups,
    and political traditions.
-   A plural and diverse society cannot easily be represented by one
    narrow definition of national identity.
-   The Constitution's Indian character can be assessed through its
    response to India's conditions, its democratic commitments, and its
    protection of diversity.

**Analytical point:** Constitutional originality does not require every
provision to be invented domestically; adaptation to local needs can
itself be an expression of originality.

## 7. Government of India Act, 1935: Structure Versus Spirit

-   The Government of India Act, 1935, influenced several administrative
    and federal arrangements.
-   However, institutional or structural continuity does not mean the
    Constitution retained the same political purpose.
-   The Constitution established India as a **sovereign democratic
    republic**, provided for universal adult franchise, guaranteed
    Fundamental Rights, and made constitutional remedies available.
-   Thus, the colonial origins of some administrative mechanisms should
    be distinguished from the democratic and rights-based framework
    established by the Constitution.

## 8. Criticism: The Constitution Is Too Lengthy and Complex

### Criticism

-   The Constitution is detailed and uses technical legal language.
-   Sir Ivor Jennings is commonly associated with describing it as a
    **"lawyer's paradise."**
-   D. D. Basu used the phrase **"beautiful patchwork"** in discussing
    the Constitution's combination of provisions and influences.

### Counterarguments

-   Detail can improve clarity and reduce ambiguity.
-   It can provide safeguards against arbitrary government action.
-   India's size, diversity, administrative needs, and complex
    Centre--State relations required careful constitutional
    arrangements.
-   The Constitution sought to provide a common framework for the Union
    and the States, while also detailing institutions and procedures.

### Why was it so bulky?

-   The original Constitution contained **395 Articles, 22 Parts, and 8
    Schedules**.
-   The makers drew on experience from several constitutional systems.
-   They included extensive administrative provisions and detailed rules
    for Centre--State relations.
-   India needed to accommodate substantial linguistic, religious,
    cultural, and regional diversity.
-   Unlike the United States, where states have their own constitutions
    alongside the federal Constitution, India uses one national
    Constitution as the principal constitutional framework for both
    Union and States. Jammu and Kashmir's former special constitutional
    arrangement was altered in 2019.

## High-Yield Terms

-   **Universal adult franchise:** The right of all eligible adult
    citizens to vote, subject to lawful qualifications.
-   **Indirect election:** Voters elect representatives who then elect
    or select members of another body.
-   **Intelligent adaptation:** Modifying ideas or institutions borrowed
    from elsewhere to fit local conditions.
-   **Constitutional remedies:** Legal means to approach courts to
    enforce Fundamental Rights.
-   **Pluralism:** Recognition and accommodation of different
    identities, beliefs, and interests within society.

    nominated.
-   **Congress:** dominant but ideologically diverse.
-   **Religious composition:** majority status does not by itself
    determine constitutional character.
-   **Borrowing:** selective borrowing plus adaptation, not necessarily
    blind copying.
-   **1935 Act:** influenced structure; constitutional goals and rights
    framework changed fundamentally.
