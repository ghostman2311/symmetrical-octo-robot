# Indian Constitution: Criticisms and the “Borrowed Constitution” Debate

**Study time:** 20–30 minutes  
**Topic:** Criticisms of the Constitution and responses to them

## 1. Criticism: The Constitution Was Hindu-Dominated

One criticism is that the Constituent Assembly was dominated by Hindus and therefore the Constitution might reflect Hindu customs, values and ancient Hindu political traditions.

### Lecturer’s argument
- The lecturer argues that the Constitution does not establish a distinctly Hindu system of polity or make Hindu customs the general basis of governance.
- Hindu nationalist and traditionalist critics at the time also argued that the Constitution was too secular and did not adequately incorporate ancient Hindu political ideas.
- Article 48, a Directive Principle concerning the organisation of agriculture and animal husbandry and the preservation of breeds, asks the State to take steps to prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle.

### Balanced understanding
- The Constitution’s secular character and the historical influence of Hindu-majority politics are subjects of scholarly debate.
- Article 48 is relevant to debates about cow protection, but its presence alone does not settle the wider question of whether the Constitution was Hindu-dominated.
- For an exam answer, explain the criticism and the counterargument rather than presenting either as an uncontested fact.

## 2. Criticism: The Constitution Was Borrowed or “Un-Indian”

Critics described the Constitution as a borrowed document, a “slavish imitation of the West,” or a carbon copy of the Government of India Act, 1935.

### Why the Government of India Act, 1935 mattered
The Act provided a major administrative and constitutional framework during British rule. Several institutional and administrative features continued in modified form after independence.

Examples of features associated with the Act include:
- Federal and administrative structures
- The office of Governor
- Public Service Commissions
- Elements of the judiciary and emergency administration

The Constitution also drew ideas from other constitutional systems. For example, parliamentary government is associated with the British model, Fundamental Rights with the US Constitution, and Directive Principles of State Policy with the Irish Constitution.

## 3. Counterargument: Borrowing Does Not Mean Blind Copying

- Constitutional ideas and institutions are not the exclusive property of one country.
- The Constituent Assembly debated provisions and adapted them to Indian conditions.
- The Government of India Act, 1935 influenced the Constitution’s administrative structure, but the Constitution also established its own framework of rights, democratic government and constitutional accountability.
- The Constitution’s broader purpose includes democratic self-government, justice, liberty, equality and the welfare of the people.

**UPSC-ready conclusion:** The Indian Constitution is a synthesis of borrowed institutional ideas and adaptations made through the Constituent Assembly’s deliberations. Its sources should be acknowledged, but describing it as merely a carbon copy overlooks the debates, modifications and constitutional principles that shaped the final document.

## Quick Revision

- **Government of India Act:** 1935
- **Article related to cow protection:** Article 48, a Directive Principle of State Policy
- **British constitutional influence:** Parliamentary system
- **US constitutional influence:** Fundamental Rights
- **Irish constitutional influence:** Directive Principles of State Policy
- **Core response to the “borrowed Constitution” criticism:** Borrowing is not the same as blind copying.
