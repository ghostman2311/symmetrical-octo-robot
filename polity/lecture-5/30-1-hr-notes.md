# Indian Constitution: Intelligent Adaptation and Major Criticisms
*UPSC revision notes | Lecture segment: 30 minutes*

## 1. Intelligent Adaptation

The Constituent Assembly did not copy foreign constitutions blindly. It **selected, modified and adapted** provisions to suit Indian conditions. Granville Austin describes this as an exercise in the “art of selection and modification.” The Constitution was influenced by other countries, but its framers adapted borrowed ideas to India's needs. [Source: Granville Austin, *The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation*](https://www.bbau.ac.in/Docs/FoundationCourse/TM/MPDC405/GRANVILLECONSTITU.pdf)

### Example: Fundamental Rights
- The US Constitution's **Bill of Rights** influenced the protection of Fundamental Rights in **Part III** of the Indian Constitution.
- India did not adopt every US right. For example, the US Second Amendment protects the right to keep and bear arms; India has no equivalent fundamental right to bear arms.
- The example illustrates selective borrowing, not wholesale copying.

**Key phrase:** *Borrowed provisions were adapted to Indian conditions.*

## 2. Why Borrow from Other Constitutions?

- **Workability:** The framers could learn from constitutional arrangements already tested elsewhere rather than experiment with every feature from scratch.
- **Shared democratic principles:** Liberty, equality, democracy and social justice were discussed during India's freedom struggle; they were not suddenly invented when the Constituent Assembly met.
- **Historical context:** A constitution drafted in the mid-20th century could reasonably share principles with other modern democratic constitutions, while differing in its specific arrangements.
- **No monopoly on constitutional ideas:** Constitutional principles are not the exclusive property of any one country.

D.D. Basu famously characterised the Constitution as a “patchwork,” but the lecture stresses that borrowing can still produce a coherent and effective framework.

## 3. Is the Constitution “Un-Indian” or a “Slavish Imitation of the West”?

This criticism is challenged on several grounds:

1. **Indian principles had developed over time.** Constitutional ideals such as liberty, equality, democracy and social welfare were debated during the freedom movement and in political forums including Congress sessions.
2. **Adaptation matters more than origin alone.** A provision's foreign origin does not mean it was copied without change.
3. **“Indian” is difficult to define narrowly.** Granville Austin's argument, as presented in the lecture, is that condemning the Constitution as “un-Indian” requires a clear definition of what counts as Indian. India's diversity makes a single, uncontested checklist difficult.
4. **Structure and purpose can differ.** Some administrative structures came from British-era laws, particularly the Government of India Act, 1935. However, independent India's Constitution established a democratic framework with Fundamental Rights and social-welfare objectives that differed substantially from colonial rule.

**Balanced conclusion:** The Constitution drew on foreign and colonial precedents, but the Constituent Assembly exercised choice and adaptation. It is more accurate to describe it as a constitution shaped by multiple influences and adapted for India than as a slavish copy.

## 4. Criticism: The Constitution Is Too Complicated

The criticism has some merit: it is a legal document, uses technical language and contains detailed provisions. Sir Ivor Jennings is associated with the description of India as a “lawyer's paradise.”

However, legal precision can help reduce ambiguity and disputes about how provisions should operate.

## 5. Why Is the Indian Constitution So Lengthy and Detailed?

At commencement, it contained **395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules**. [Official source: Digital Sansad](https://sansad.in/ls/about/introduction)

Main reasons:

1. **Accumulated constitutional experience:** The framers drew on the experience of other constitutions, including lessons from arrangements that worked or failed.
2. **Administrative detail:** It covers matters such as public services, elections, auditing, public accounts and official languages, not only broad constitutional principles.
3. **One constitutional framework:** Unlike the United States, where each state has its own constitution, India has a single Constitution for the Union and the States.
4. **India's diversity:** Detailed provisions helped address varied regional, social and administrative circumstances.
5. **Centre–State relations:** The Constitution sets out federal powers and responsibilities in considerable detail.
6. **Clarity:** Longer provisions can specify conditions, exceptions and procedures, helping limit uncertainty. Excessive detail, however, can also make a constitution harder to read.

### Important correction
Article 370 was **made inoperative in its earlier form through constitutional and parliamentary measures in 2019**; saying simply that it was “abrogated” is common shorthand but leaves out the legal process. This is a separate topic and should be studied in detail later. [PIB, 5 August 2019](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1581308&lang=1&reg=3)

## Quick Revision

- **Intelligent adaptation:** Select, modify and tailor foreign provisions to Indian conditions.
- **Fundamental Rights:** Part III; influenced in part by the US Bill of Rights.
- **Granville Austin:** Emphasised selection and modification of borrowed constitutional provisions.
- **Jennings:** Associated with the phrase “lawyer's paradise.”
- **Original Constitution:** 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules.
- **Length and detail:** Administrative provisions, diversity, federal relations, a single constitutional framework and the aim of clarity.
