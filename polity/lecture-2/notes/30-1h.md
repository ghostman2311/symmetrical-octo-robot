# Significance of the Constitution — UPSC Notes (Lecture Continuation)

**One-line summary:** The Constitution structures the polity, governs relationships within the State and between the State and people, expresses ideals and responds to social conflicts, unites diversity, legitimises State action and places enforceable limits on government.

**Transcript range:** 00:30:04–01:00:06. This is a continuation of the previous lecture segment; the teacher's first aspect, the Constitution as the “birth certificate” of a nation, was covered earlier.

## 1. Topic + One-line Summary

The lecture continues the **significance of the Constitution**, moving from the basic structure of the polity to its role in reflecting ideals and conflicts, promoting unity, legitimising State action and limiting government power.

## 2. Core Notes

### Second aspect — Basic structure of the polity and relationships

The teacher develops three dimensions:

1. **Structure:** The legislature, executive and judiciary are the basic institutions.
2. **Inter se relationship:** The Constitution governs relationships **among these three institutions** (*inter se* = among themselves).
3. **Relationship with people:**
   - The **Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies** are elected by the people.
   - Under **Part III**, the State must not violate rights such as life, liberty, occupation/trade and freedom of religion.
   - Under **Part IV**, the State should endeavour to promote equitable development, employment, agriculture and the welfare of the poor.
   - If the State violates rights, an affected person can approach the **judiciary**.

**Meaning of “State”:** The teacher uses it broadly for organised government at Union, State and local levels—not merely a territorial State such as Maharashtra. This is why the State–people relationship concerns all relevant public authorities.

### Third aspect — Ideals, aspirations and conflicts

**Ideal vs aspiration**
- **Ideal:** A principle or norm to follow.
- **Aspiration:** The objective society wants to achieve.
- The same value can be both an ideal and an aspiration.

**Examples**
- **Democracy:** Democratic processes are an ideal; becoming a fully functioning democracy is an aspiration. Finland, Sweden and Denmark are used as aspirational comparisons [teacher's view; no index ranks are specified].
- **Secularism:** The State should not identify with a religion and people should not face religious discrimination. Continuing majority and minority communal tendencies show the gap between ideal and aspiration.
- **Justice, liberty and equality:** Each can be an ideal and an aspiration. Political freedom from foreign rule does not automatically mean freedom from caste or communal oppression, patriarchy, corruption or poverty. The teacher also refers to poverty-related setbacks after COVID-19.

**The Constitution also reflects conflicts in society**
- **Name — Article 1:** “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.” The two names illustrate the Constituent Assembly debate and compromise over the country's name. Bhutan/Druk (Land of the Thunder Dragon) and Japan/Nippon are mentioned as examples of countries with more than one name.
- **Language — Article 343:** Hindi was made the **official language of the Union**, not the national language. English was initially to continue for 15 years and its use continued beyond that period. The debate reflected concerns, particularly in southern States, about elevating one language above others; the teacher refers to intense linguistic politics in the 1950s and 1960s.
- **Caste and religion:** Caste hierarchy and untouchability historically ostracised Dalits and obstructed social, educational, economic and political development. Religious-community tensions created insecurity among minorities.
- **Constitutional response:** The teacher points to special provisions and concessions for **SCs, STs and OBCs**, including reservations/quotas, and refers to provisions for minorities and women (details deferred).
- The Constitution therefore acts as a **mirror of society**: it reflects conflicts and attempts to address historical injustice.
- **Article 1 petitions:** The teacher mentions petitions seeking removal of “India” from Article 1. His point is that a court cannot itself rewrite the constitutional text; a formal change would require the constitutional amendment process. The outcome of a specific petition is clarified in Additions.

### Fourth aspect — Constitution as a common normative home and instrument of unity

- India is described as a **“melting pot of races”** with diversity in languages and dialects, religions, castes and sub-castes, regions, customs, dress, food habits and festivals.
- The teacher's rough illustration is that a language or dialect may change around every **200 kilometres** [teacher's view; approximate].
- Examples: **Durga Puja in West Bengal** is described as almost at par with Diwali in importance there, while it is not celebrated with the same fervour among many Punjabi Hindus; Diwali is identified as a principal festival for many North Indian Hindus. Practices vary even within the same religious community.
- Diverse groups can live as one nation when they accept **minimum common principles of coexistence**. The Constitution provides this shared set of norms and acts as a common normative home and instrument of unity.

**Historical analogy — Ashoka's Dhamma**
- Ashoka's moral precepts, influenced by Buddhist ideas, were spread through edicts across the subcontinent; the teacher describes his empire as the largest India had seen and as extending up to Afghanistan [teacher's characterisation].
- Society included Vedic/Brahmanical traditions, Buddhists, Jains, Ajivikas and tribal communities.
- The interpretation attributed to **Romila Thapar** is that Dhamma was not merely religious missionary activity; it also functioned as a political instrument for promoting common norms and unity.
- The analogy is that Dhamma offered shared moral principles across diverse groups, just as the Constitution provides common principles for India's diverse society.

### Next significance point — Constitution gives legitimacy to the State and its actions

The examples explain why people recognise institutional authority even when they may dispute an individual decision:

- **Judiciary:** A judge issues an arrest warrant and the police execute it. A person may dispute the facts or fairness of the arrest but recognises the court's authority to issue the warrant.
- **Legislature and executive:** Parliament makes laws; a District Magistrate may issue orders under **Section 144 of the CrPC**, including restrictions on assemblies of five or more persons in specified circumstances.
- **Taxation:** Citizens may dislike a tax but recognise the government's authority to levy and collect it.

**Central argument:** The legitimacy of State power and action derives from the **Constitution**, not simply from the individual occupying an office.

### Fifth point — A democratic Constitution places legally enforceable limits on government

- In a democracy, government cannot have **absolute authority**; the teacher contrasts this with autocracy [teacher's example: North Korea].
- The Constitution places **legally enforceable limitations** on government power.
- Example: The State cannot take away protected rights at will; a violation can be challenged before a court.
- The teacher begins to refer to **affirmative action and special benefits**, but the explanation is cut off.

## 8. Additions (Not from Lecture)

Each item below is marked [+] in the notes or is an extra clarification of a statement made in the lecture.

- **[+] Article 343 detail:** Article 343(1) specifies Hindi in Devanagari script as the official language of the Union. Article 343(2) provided for English to continue for 15 years from commencement; Article 343(3) allows Parliament to provide by law for use of English after that period. **Reason:** The lecture gives the 15-year period and says English was continued, but does not explain the exact clauses. **Source:** Constitution of India, Article 343; Legislative Department's official Constitution text.

- **[+] Continuation of English:** Section 3 of the Official Languages Act, 1963 provides for the continued use of English in addition to Hindi for specified Union official purposes and for parliamentary business after the 15-year period. **Reason:** This supplies the law behind the lecture's statement that English continued beyond 15 years. **Source:** India Code, Official Languages Act, 1963, Section 3.

- **[+] No national language declared:** The Constitution identifies Hindi as the official language of the Union under Article 343; it does not designate a national language. **Reason:** This clarifies the lecture's distinction between “official language” and “national language”. **Source:** Constitution of India, Article 343; Department of Official Language, Government of India.

- **[+] PIL concerning Article 1:** On 3 June 2020, the Supreme Court disposed of a petition seeking replacement of “India” in Article 1 with “Bharat”, directing that it be treated as a representation for consideration by the appropriate Ministries. **Reason:** The lecture says the Court had not responded to such petitions; this example shows that at least one petition was disposed of in this manner rather than left unanswered. **Source:** Supreme Court order in W.P. (C) No. 422/2020, as reported by Indian Express and India Today.

- **[+] Current criminal-procedure reference — [DATED - verify current]:** The lecture's Section 144 reference is to the former Code of Criminal Procedure. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 came into force on 1 July 2024; Section 163 now deals with urgent cases of nuisance or apprehended danger. **Reason:** This updates the legal reference for current exam preparation. **Source:** India Code, BNSS, 2023, Section 163 and commencement details.

- **[+] Ashoka's Dhamma:** The National Museum describes Ashoka's Dhamma as a moral and social code and notes that his edicts emphasised peace and amity among religious sects. Romila Thapar's work discusses Dhamma as an attempt to promote social order and broad ethical conduct across cultural groups. **Reason:** This supports the lecture's historical analogy while preserving the fact that it is an interpretation of Dhamma, not a constitutional rule. **Sources:** National Museum, Edicts of Ashoka; Romila Thapar, *Aśoka and the Decline of the Mauryas*, Oxford University Press.

- **[+] Sarkaria Commission detail:** The Commission's recommended criteria also included that a Governor should be eminent, detached from local politics and not too involved in politics in the recent past. **Reason:** The lecture specifically mentions the outside-State recommendation; these are additional details from the Commission's report. **Source:** Sarkaria Commission Report, Chapter on the Role of the Governor and Centre-State Relations.
