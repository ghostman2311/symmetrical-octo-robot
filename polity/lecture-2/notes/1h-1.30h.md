# Rule of Law, Equality Before Law and Constitutional Immunities — UPSC Notes

**One-line summary:** Dicey's rule of law emphasises equality before law and ordinary courts, but modern governance, substantive equality and limited constitutional or diplomatic immunities show that its principles must be understood in context.

## 1. Topic + One-line Summary

This lecture segment continues the discussion of **Rule of Law**, focusing on formal and substantive equality, ordinary courts and quasi-judicial forums, immunities of constitutional office-holders and diplomats, and criminal defamation.

## 2. Core Notes

### A. Equality Before Law: Formal and Substantive Equality

- **Formal equality:** All persons are subject to the same law; the same criminal offence should attract the same legal treatment, irrespective of religion, caste, class or wealth. [+] This equality principle is constitutionally anchored in Article 14.
- **Substantive equality:** Identical treatment may perpetuate real disadvantage. Special benefits for disadvantaged/backward groups are used in democratic governance to help achieve actual equality.
- **Teacher's illustration:** In a race between an elephant and a cheetah, making both start at the same point does not create a genuinely equal contest. Giving the elephant a **50-metre head start** is a special concession intended to reduce the unequal starting conditions.
- The teacher describes this as **“discrimination to end discrimination”**: differential treatment may be used to achieve substantive equality.

### B. Dicey's Rule of Law: Equality Through Ordinary Courts

- The teacher discusses A.V. Dicey's formulation of the **Rule of Law**, developed roughly 150 years ago.
- The teacher briefly recaps the first element as **primacy of law**. The next element discussed is that everyone is equally subject to the law as administered through **ordinary courts**.
- The teacher identifies the ordinary court structure as the **Supreme Court, High Courts, district courts and subordinate courts**.
- Modern administration also uses **quasi-judicial forums/tribunals** that perform adjudicatory functions. Examples in the lecture:
  - Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT).
  - Income Tax Appellate Tribunal.
  - Consumer dispute forums/commissions.
  - Information Commissions hearing appeals against denial of information.
- The teacher's argument is that Dicey's older formulation could not fully anticipate the complexity of modern administration. Law is still largely administered by ordinary courts, but specialised forums also perform necessary adjudicatory functions.

### C. Exception to Formal Equality: President and Governor

- The teacher explains that criminal law generally requires equal treatment: the same offence should not attract different punishment merely because the offender belongs to a different religion, caste, class or economic group.
- The Constitution nevertheless provides a special protection to the **President and Governors while they hold office**.
- The transcript's garbled article reference is corrected to **Article 361**: no criminal proceedings may be instituted or continued against the President or a Governor during the term of office.
  - [+] Article 361 also bars a court from issuing a process for their arrest or imprisonment during that term.
  - [+] These protections appear specifically in clauses 361(2) and 361(3), respectively.
- **Dignity-of-office argument:** One justification offered in the classroom is protection of the dignity of the high office. The teacher questions whether this explanation is sufficient: the CJI, Chief Election Commissioner, CAG and Prime Minister also hold high offices, but do not receive the same protection. The teacher argues that allowing a person facing clear criminal allegations to remain unprosecuted may itself erode the office's dignity. **[teacher's view]**
- The teacher invokes Dr B.R. Ambedkar's description of the CAG as an especially important constitutional officer to reinforce this comparison.

### D. Critique Attributed in the Lecture to “Noorani” [VERIFY]

The lecture presents three criticisms of the immunity provision:

1. **Borrowing from British practice:** The provision is said to resemble the immunity enjoyed by the British Crown. The argument presented is that British courts were historically understood as Crown courts/institutions created by the Crown. In India, however, the President and courts are both creations of the Constitution; therefore, the same rationale does not transfer neatly. The speaker characterises this borrowing as mindless imitation. **[teacher's view / attribution VERIFY]**
2. **Possibility of abuse:** A politician facing criminal prosecution could allegedly be appointed Governor to delay proceedings during the Governor's tenure. The teacher refers to unnamed reports/controversies in which public pressure was followed by removal from office and prosecution, but does not identify the individuals. These examples remain **[VERIFY]** and are not treated here as established facts.
3. **Reverse-engineering argument:** The speaker asks readers to imagine that this immunity did not exist and a political party proposed a constitutional amendment to introduce it today. The predicted public, media and judicial opposition is used to question the legitimacy of retaining such a provision. **[teacher's view]**

### E. Return to Dicey: Other Limits and Exceptions

- The lecture again summarises the relevant aspects of Dicey's Rule of Law: equality before law and administration through ordinary courts.
- **Diplomatic immunity:** Foreign diplomats, ambassadors and high commissioners enjoy immunity from criminal prosecution in the receiving State. The teacher explains that the receiving State may seek their return/expulsion and hopes that the sending country will take appropriate action under its own law, on a **reciprocal basis**. The teacher treats diplomatic immunity as a justified exception to formal equality. [+] Under Article 31 of the Vienna Convention, immunity from receiving-State criminal jurisdiction does not exempt the diplomat from the jurisdiction of the sending State.
- **Section 144 CrPC:** The District Magistrate's power to restrict gatherings (the lecture mentions a restriction on gatherings of more than five persons) is used as an example of statutory authority exercised by the administration.
- **Freedom of speech and defamation:** Freedom of speech does not permit a person to defame another. The lecture refers to **Section 499 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860**, which defined criminal defamation. [+] Article 19(2) expressly lists defamation as a ground for reasonable restrictions on speech. [+] Section 500 IPC prescribed the punishment. The teacher's view is that defamation should be a civil wrong attracting monetary compensation rather than imprisonment, but notes that Indian law treated it as a criminal offence. **[teacher's view on the desirability of civil-only treatment]**

## 8. Additions (Not from Lecture)

- **[+] Article 14:** The constitutional provision most directly associated with equality before law and equal protection of laws is Article 14. *Reason:* The lecture discusses equality before law but does not state the Article number. **Source type:** Constitution of India, Article 14.
- **[+] Article 361 clause references:** The criminal-proceeding protection is in Article 361(2), and the bar on court process for arrest or imprisonment is in Article 361(3). *Reason:* The teacher gives the protection but the clause numbers are additional precision. **Source type:** Constitution of India, Article 361.
- **[+] Article 361 nuance:** The immunity is personal to the President/Governor; it does not automatically prevent judicial examination of the validity of governmental action taken in the Governor's name. *Reason:* This qualification helps avoid treating personal immunity as total immunity of the State's action. **Source type:** Supreme Court judgment, *Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006).
- **[+] Section 500, IPC:** Section 499 defined defamation, while Section 500 prescribed its punishment under the former IPC. *Reason:* The lecture refers to Section 499 but does not clearly state the separate punishment provision. **Source type:** Indian Penal Code, 1860, Sections 499–500.
- **[+] Diplomatic immunity detail:** Article 31(1) of the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations provides immunity from the criminal jurisdiction of the receiving State; Article 31(4) clarifies that this does not exempt the diplomat from the jurisdiction of the sending State. Article 32 deals with waiver of immunity. *Reason:* The lecture describes immunity and possible return to the sending country but does not provide the Convention's Article numbers or the continuing jurisdiction principle. **Source type:** Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, 1961; Diplomatic Relations (Vienna Convention) Act, 1972.
- **[+] Article 19(2):** Defamation is expressly listed as a ground on which reasonable restrictions may be imposed on the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a). *Reason:* The lecture discusses defamation as a limit on speech but does not name Article 19(2). **Source type:** Constitution of India, Article 19(2).
- **[DATED - verify current] [+] Current criminal defamation provision:** The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 came into force on 1 July 2024. Defamation is now covered by Section 356 BNS, which carries criminal consequences; the former IPC Sections 499 and 500 are the lecture-era references. *Reason:* The teacher cites the IPC, which is no longer the current general criminal code for offences committed after commencement of the BNS. **Source type:** India Code, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023; commencement notification.
- **[DATED - verify current] [+] Current equivalent of Section 144 CrPC:** The urgent-order provision corresponding to the lecture's Section 144 CrPC example is Section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, in force from 1 July 2024. *Reason:* The lecture uses the former CrPC provision; current statutory references have changed. **Source type:** India Code, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.
- **[+] Vienna Convention's domestic legal effect:** India gave the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations legal effect through the Diplomatic Relations (Vienna Convention) Act, 1972. *Reason:* The lecture does not name the Indian implementing Act. **Source type:** Diplomatic Relations (Vienna Convention) Act, 1972, Ministry of External Affairs.
