# Constitution and Its Basics — UPSC Notes

**One-line summary:** The Constitution is the supreme and fundamental law of a country; it establishes the basic structure of governance, while constitutional law also includes amendments, judicial interpretations and conventions.

## 1. Topic and Summary

The lecture covers three connected themes:

1. Meaning of the **Constitution** and its difference from ordinary or statutory law.
2. Meaning of **constitutional law**, including conventions.
3. Significance of the Constitution in establishing the polity and its institutions.

## 2. Core Notes

### A. Meaning of the Constitution

**Why “a body of laws” is an incomplete definition**

- A Constitution contains laws in its Articles, provisions and Schedules.
- Laws also exist outside the Constitution. The teacher gives the examples of the **Right to Information Act, 2005**, consumer-dispute legislation and the law establishing the **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)**.
- These ordinary laws are made by legislative bodies: **Parliament** at the Union level and **State Legislatures** at the State level.
- The Constitution itself creates these legislative bodies and gives them law-making authority. Parliament has two Houses; a State Legislature has an Assembly and, in some States, a Legislative Council as well.

**Constitution as the supreme and fundamental law**

- The Constitution is the **fundamental law** containing the basic principles according to which a politically organised society is governed. Its most common manifestation is the **nation-state**.
- Parliament and State Legislatures must exercise their law-making powers within the limits of the Constitution. Ordinary laws made outside the Constitution cannot violate its fundamental provisions.
- The Constitution is therefore described as the **Supreme Law of the Land** or the “law of laws”.
- The teacher notes that many countries emerging from colonial rule first framed a Constitution to establish the basic principles by which the independent nation would be governed. He mentions Portuguese, Spanish, French and British colonial rule as examples of colonial backgrounds.

**Constitutional law vs. statutory law**

- **Constitutional law:** The fundamental law contained in the Constitution.
- **Statutory law:** Ordinary laws made by Parliament or State Legislatures outside the Constitution.
- Constitutional law is more difficult to change: the teacher contrasts the special-majority procedure for constitutional amendments with the simple-majority procedure generally used for ordinary legislation.
- The Constitution should not be so easily changed that a government with a small parliamentary majority can alter it at will; otherwise, its fundamental status may be weakened.

### B. Constitutional Law — Wider than the Constitution

The Constitution is only a **subset of constitutional law**. The teacher identifies four components:

1. **The Constitution:** The original constitutional provisions.
2. **Constitutional amendments:** Subsequent changes made to the Constitution.
3. **Case law:** Interpretations of constitutional provisions by the Supreme Court and High Courts. The Supreme Court is the final interpreter of the Constitution. Courts may interpret a similar provision differently at different points in time.
4. **Conventions:** Unwritten practices that guide the functioning of constitutional institutions.

### C. Fourth Component — Constitutional Conventions

**Meaning**

- Conventions are customary ways of doing things that become accepted through consistent practice and wider public or social acceptance.
- They are not written or codified in the Constitution or a statute, but are followed as established norms. The teacher describes them as having the “force of law” in the sense that people follow them as accepted practices.
- Their violation may produce social or political consequences rather than ordinary legal penalties. The teacher illustrates this with the example of greeting friends by shaking hands: refusing to greet one friend while greeting others may strain the relationship, but it does not attract a legal penalty.
- A practice routinely violated does not become an established convention; wider acceptance is necessary.

**Teacher's examples of constitutional conventions**

1. **Government formation:** The leader of a party or coalition able to command a majority in the Lok Sabha or State Legislative Assembly is conventionally invited by the President or Governor, respectively, to form the government.
2. **Loss of majority:** A Prime Minister or Chief Minister who loses majority support is expected to step down. The teacher connects this expectation with the possibility of losing a vote of no-confidence.
3. **Governor from outside the State:** The Sarkaria Commission recommended that a Governor should preferably be appointed from outside the State. The reasoning was that a politician from the same State may know its political dynamics closely and may be tempted to interfere in State politics; an outsider may be less inclined to do so. **[teacher's view]** The teacher assesses that this convention has been followed more or less, but that its intended objective has been only partially achieved.

**When the Constitution is silent**

The teacher identifies two ways to address an unaddressed constitutional situation:

- **Codification:** Write the requirement into the Constitution.
- **Convention:** Allow an accepted constitutional practice to develop.

The teacher says the detailed question of why an established convention is not simply codified will be discussed later.

### D. Significance of the Constitution

**First — Birth certificate of a nation**

- When a country becomes independent, it needs a basic body of law establishing the principles according to which it will be governed.
- The teacher compares the Constitution to a child's birth certificate: **“What a birth certificate is to a child, a Constitution is to a nation.”**
- It marks the emergence of a sovereign nation and establishes its basic governing principles.

**Second — Provides the basic structure of the polity**

- **Polity** means the system, form or process of governance of a country.
- Polity is wider than the Constitution because governance also operates through statutory laws and institutions outside the Constitution.
- The Constitution establishes the basic institutional structure of governance through three principal functions:
  - **Legislature:** Makes laws.
  - **Executive:** Implements laws.
  - **Judiciary:** Performs judicial functions.

**Three types of institutions in India**

- **Constitutional bodies:** Created directly by the Constitution. Examples given: UPSC, Election Commission and National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC). The transcript also mentions a “127th Amendment Bill” in connection with the NCBC; the intended connection is unclear [VERIFY].
- **Statutory bodies:** Created through legislation passed by Parliament or a State Legislature. Examples given: NHRC, Information Commissions and Consumer Disputes Forums.
- **Executive bodies:** Created by the Union or State executive without a specific constitutional provision or separate statute establishing them. Examples given: Planning Commission, NITI Aayog and National Development Council. The teacher says the Planning Commission was replaced by NITI Aayog and that the National Development Council had been sidelined.

## 3. Teacher's Warnings and Traps

- Defining the Constitution merely as a **body of laws** is a simplistic definition; it fails to distinguish constitutional law from ordinary laws outside the Constitution.
- Defining constitutional law as only the provisions written in the Constitution is incomplete; it also includes amendments, case law and conventions.
