## 5. Mains Practice Questions

### Question 1 — Criticism + Qualification

**Q1.** Explain the criticism that defining the Constitution merely as a body of laws is inadequate. What arguments qualify or counter this definition?

**Model Answer (maximum 250 words)**

**Introduction:** A Constitution contains legal provisions, but describing it merely as a body of laws does not explain its unique status in a country's legal system.

**Body**

- **Criticism — Incomplete definition:** Ordinary statutes also contain laws. The Right to Information Act, 2005 is an example of a law outside the Constitution.
- **Qualification — Fundamental law:** The Constitution establishes the basic principles according to which a politically organised society is governed.
- **Qualification — Institutional authority:** The Constitution itself creates Parliament and State Legislatures and gives them the authority to make ordinary laws.
- **Qualification — Supremacy:** Ordinary laws made by these legislative bodies cannot violate the Constitution, which is the Supreme Law of the Land.

**Conclusion:** The Constitution is certainly a body of laws, but its defining feature is its fundamental and supreme status. It provides the framework and limits within which ordinary law-making takes place.

**Keywords to hit:** Fundamental law, Supreme Law, statutory law, legislative authority, Constitution, supremacy.

**Common mistake:** Defining the Constitution only by its contents without explaining its superior legal status.

### Question 2 — Concept Contrast

**Q2.** What is the difference between the Constitution and constitutional law?

**Model Answer (maximum 150 words)**

**Introduction:** The Constitution is the core framework of a country's governance, whereas constitutional law is a wider body of law concerning its interpretation and operation.

**Body**

- **Constitution:** Contains the fundamental provisions according to which the nation is governed.
- **Amendments:** Subsequent amendments to the Constitution also form part of constitutional law.
- **Case law:** Judicial interpretations of constitutional provisions by the Supreme Court and High Courts form part of constitutional law.
- **Conventions:** Unwritten practices that guide constitutional institutions are also included in constitutional law.

**Conclusion:** The Constitution is the central component of constitutional law, but constitutional law extends beyond the constitutional text to include amendments, judicial interpretations and conventions.

**Keywords to hit:** Constitution, constitutional law, amendments, case law, conventions, interpretation.

**Common mistake:** Treating constitutional law and the Constitution as exactly the same thing.

### Question 3 — Quoted Statement + Discuss

**Q3.** “Constitutional conventions are unwritten practices that guide constitutional functioning, but they do not carry the same legal consequences as statutory laws.” Discuss.

**Model Answer (maximum 150 words)**

**Introduction:** Constitutional conventions are customary practices accepted over time that guide the functioning of constitutional institutions without being formally codified.

**Body**

- **Limitation:** Unlike statutory laws, conventions are not written in the Constitution or legislation and their violation does not ordinarily attract legal penalties.
- **Practical importance:** The leader who can command a majority in the Lok Sabha or State Assembly is conventionally invited to form the government by the President or Governor, respectively.
- **Democratic expectation:** A Prime Minister or Chief Minister who loses majority support is expected to step down, although the teacher notes that this expectation is not expressly codified in the Constitution.
- **Social acceptance:** The handshake example illustrates that a convention is sustained by accepted practice and may carry social consequences when disregarded.

**Conclusion:** Conventions lack the formal status of statutory laws, but they provide accepted norms that help constitutional institutions function in situations where the written Constitution is silent.

**Keywords to hit:** Constitutional conventions, unwritten practices, majority rule, President, Governor, constitutional functioning.

**Common mistake:** Assuming that an unwritten convention is legally enforceable in the same way as a statute.

### Question 4 — Critical Evaluation

**Q4.** Does describing the Constitution as the “birth certificate of a nation” fully explain its significance? Explain critically.

**Model Answer (maximum 250 words)**

**Introduction:** The Constitution marks the emergence of a sovereign nation by establishing the fundamental principles according to which it will be governed. However, its significance extends beyond this symbolic role.

**Body**

- **National identity:** The Constitution is compared to a birth certificate because a newly independent country establishes its basic governing principles through a Constitution.
- **Polity structure:** The Constitution provides the basic institutional structure of governance by establishing the legislature, executive and judiciary.
- **Functional division:** The legislature makes laws, the executive implements them and the judiciary performs judicial functions.
- **Institutional framework:** The Constitution establishes constitutional bodies, while statutory and executive bodies also contribute to governance.
- **Wider governance system:** Polity includes not only constitutional provisions but also statutory laws and institutions outside the Constitution.

**Conclusion:** The birth-certificate analogy captures the Constitution's role in establishing a sovereign nation, but its deeper significance lies in structuring the country's institutions and system of governance.

**Keywords to hit:** Sovereign nation, birth certificate, polity, legislature, executive, judiciary.

**Common mistake:** Limiting the Constitution's significance to the formal establishment of an independent country.
