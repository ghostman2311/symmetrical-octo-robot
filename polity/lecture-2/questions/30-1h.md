## 5. Mains Practice Questions

### Q1 — Criticism + Qualification (maximum 250 words)

**Question:** Explain the criticism that a common constitutional framework may be insufficient to unite a deeply diverse society. What arguments qualify or counter this criticism?

**Model answer**

**Introduction:**  
India's diversity of language, religion, caste, region and custom poses a challenge to national integration. A common constitutional text may appear insufficient unless people accept shared principles.

**Body**

- **Diversity:** The lecture describes differences in languages, customs, food habits and festivals, including regional differences in the celebration of Durga Puja and Diwali.
- **Common norms:** The Constitution supplies minimum principles of coexistence to which diverse groups can subscribe.
- **Social justice:** Special provisions and reservations for SCs, STs and OBCs address historical exclusion and inequality.
- **Historical analogy:** Ashoka's Dhamma is presented as an earlier attempt to promote common moral principles among different religious and social groups.

**Conclusion:**  
A Constitution cannot erase diversity, but it can provide a shared normative framework and mechanisms to address conflict. Its unifying role depends on acceptance and implementation of those common principles.

**Keywords to hit:** Diversity, coexistence, common norms, social justice, reservations, unity.

**Common mistake:** Claiming that national unity requires cultural or linguistic uniformity.

### Q2 — Concept Contrast (maximum 150 words)

**Question:** What is the difference between the inter se relationship among the organs of the State and the relationship between State institutions and the people?

**Model answer**

**Introduction:**  
The Constitution regulates both the internal relationship among State institutions and their relationship with citizens.

**Body**

- **Inter se relationship:** It concerns relations among the legislature, executive and judiciary.
- **State–people relationship:** It defines what the State may or may not do to individuals.
- **Rights:** Part III protects rights such as life, liberty, occupation and religious freedom against State action.
- **State obligations:** Part IV directs the State to pursue objectives such as equitable development, employment and agricultural promotion.

**Conclusion:**  
The first relationship concerns coordination and limits among State organs; the second concerns the rights of people and the responsibilities of the State towards them.

**Keywords to hit:** Inter se, legislature, executive, judiciary, Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles.

**Common mistake:** Treating inter-institutional relations and State–citizen relations as the same constitutional relationship.

### Q3 — Quoted Statement + Discuss (maximum 150 words)

**Question:** “The Constitution expresses the ideals and aspirations of society, but it also mirrors the conflicts that society has not overcome.” Discuss.

**Model answer**

**Introduction:**  
An ideal is a principle to be followed, while an aspiration is a goal society seeks to achieve. The Constitution reflects both, alongside social conflicts.

**Body**

- **Ideals:** The Preamble expresses values such as democracy and secularism.
- **Aspirations:** A fully functioning democracy and a society free from communal discrimination remain goals to be achieved.
- **Social conflict:** Article 1 reflects the debate over the country's name, resolved through the expression “India, that is Bharat”.
- **Corrective provisions:** Reservations and special provisions for backward classes respond to historical caste-based exclusion.

**Conclusion:**  
The Constitution is both a statement of the society India seeks to build and a reflection of the inequalities and conflicts it must address.

**Keywords to hit:** Ideals, aspirations, Preamble, social conflict, Article 1, reservations.

**Common mistake:** Describing the Constitution only as a statement of achieved conditions rather than also of future goals and unresolved conflicts.

### Q4 — Critical Evaluation (maximum 250 words)

**Question:** Does the Constitution merely establish State institutions, or does it also provide legitimacy to their actions and impose enforceable limits on their powers? Explain critically.

**Model answer**

**Introduction:**  
The Constitution does more than establish institutions. It provides the source of their authority and sets legal boundaries within which they must function.

**Body**

- **Institutional authority:** A judge's authority to issue an arrest warrant is accepted because the judicial office operates within the constitutional system.
- **Executive authority:** The lecture uses Section 144 of the CrPC as an example of an executive power exercised by a District Magistrate.
- **Legitimacy:** The authority to levy and collect taxes is accepted as a function of the State, even when citizens may disagree with a particular tax.
- **Legal limits:** In a democracy, government cannot exercise absolute power; the Constitution places legally enforceable limits on State action.
- **Remedy:** A person whose protected rights are violated by the State may approach the judiciary.

**Conclusion:**  
The Constitution both legitimises public authority and restrains its exercise. Legitimacy is therefore conditional on institutions acting within constitutional limits.

**Keywords to hit:** Legitimacy, State authority, constitutional limits, judicial remedy, executive power, democracy.

**Common mistake:** Assuming that constitutional legitimacy gives State institutions unlimited authority.

### Answer Key

1. **Structure** of legislature, executive and judiciary; **inter se relations** among them; and their **relationship with the people**.
2. An ideal is a principle or norm; an aspiration is the goal to be achieved. Democracy, secularism, justice, liberty and equality can be both.
3. Article 1 reflects the naming debate through “India, that is Bharat”; Article 343 reflects the language compromise by making Hindi the official language of the Union while initially continuing English for 15 years.
4. The Constitution recognises historical caste exclusion and religious-minority concerns through special provisions and concessions, including reservations for backward classes.
5. It supplies minimum common norms of coexistence. The teacher compares this with Ashoka's Dhamma as a common moral framework across diverse groups.
6. Constitutional authority legitimises institutional action; constitutional rights and judicial remedies constrain misuse of that authority.
7. **(b) only two** — Statements 1 and 3 are correct; Hindi is the official language of the Union, not a constitutionally declared national language.
8. **(b) only two** — Statements 1 and 2 are correct; Part IV contains Directive Principles, not Fundamental Rights.
9. **(b) only two** — Pairs 1-A and 2-B are correct; pair 3-C is incorrect.
10. **(a) only one** — Only statement 1 is correct; Article 1 does not say that only Bharat may be used, and courts do not independently amend constitutional text.
11. **(b) only two** — Statements 1 and 2 are correct; the Constitution does not declare a national language.
12. **(b) only two** — Statements 1 and 3 are correct; constitutional democracy requires limits on government power.
